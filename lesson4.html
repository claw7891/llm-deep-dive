Quantization & Model Compression

How to shrink models 4× without destroying quality

A 70B-parameter model at FP32 precision requires 280 GB of GPU memory — more than a single H100 (80 GB) can hold. Yet you can run a 70B model on a single GPU if you quantize it to 4-bit (35 GB). How does dropping 75% of the precision not destroy the model? The answer lies in understanding what model weights actually encode, and how quantization exploits the structure of neural network parameters.
TL;DR

Quantization reduces the precision of model weights and/or activations, typically from 16-bit floating point to 8-bit integers or 4-bit normalized floats. Memory drops linearly with bits (4× for FP16 → 4-bit). Quality degradation depends on the method: round-to-nearest works for INT8; GPTQ, AWQ, and NF4 formats are needed for 4-bit. Weights in large models are surprisingly robust to precision loss because they have high redundancy.

Why Quantization Works

Neural network weights have two key properties that make quantization feasible:

  • Overparameterization — models have far more parameters than needed; small perturbations to individual weights don't change the output much
  • Weight distribution — most weights are small/near-zero; only a few outliers carry most of the signal

The loss landscape near a trained minimum is surprisingly flat in many directions — you can move individual weights quite far before model quality measurably degrades.

Size vs Quality Trade-off

📊 Quantization Impact: Size vs Quality

NF4 maintains ~95% of FP32 quality at 12.5% the model size. INT4 drops to ~93%. Quality measurements vary by task — reasoning benchmarks degrade faster than simple QA.

Post-Training Quantization (PTQ)

The most practical approach: take a pretrained model and quantize its weights without any retraining.

Round-to-Nearest (RTN)

The simplest method. For each weight w, find the closest representable value q in the target format:

q = clamp(round(w / s), Qmin, Qmax) · s

Where s = scale factor (max(|w|) / Qmax) and Qmin, Qmax bound the target integer range.

Works well for INT8 (negligible quality loss) but degrades significantly at 4-bit because many weights map to the same quantized value, losing distinguishing information.

GPTQ (Generative Pre-Trained Transformer Quantization)

Obtains significantly better 4-bit results by solving an optimization problem. Instead of rounding each weight independently, GPTQ adjusts remaining weights to compensate for the error introduced by quantizing earlier weights:

  1. Select a weight column to quantize
  2. Quantize it via rounding
  3. Compute the reconstruction error
  4. Update the remaining (unquantized) weights in that row to cancel the error
  5. Move to the next column

Uses a small calibration dataset (~128 sequences) to compute optimal compensation. State-of-the-art for 4-bit quantization.

AWQ (Activation-Aware Weight Quantization)

Recognizes that not all weight channels are equally important. Weights corresponding to activations with large magnitudes contribute more to output quality and should be quantized with higher precision:

  1. Compute activation statistics from calibration data
  2. Identify "salient" channels (activations with high magnitude)
  3. Scale up salient channels before quantization (preserves their precision)
  4. Scale other channels down (allows more aggressive quantization without increasing average error)

AWQ achieves similar quality to GPTQ but is faster to compute and has better hardware utilization during inference.

Quantization-Aware Training (QAT)

For the best quality at very low precision (2–3 bits), train with simulated quantization in the forward pass:

  1. Insert fake quantization nodes after each weight in the computation graph
  2. Forward pass uses quantized weights and activations
  3. Backward pass uses straight-through estimator (STE) — gradient flows through the quantization operation as if it were the identity
  4. After training, replace with real quantization

QAT is expensive (requires training from scratch or continued pretraining) but can recover most quality lost at 4-bit and below.

Summary

  • Quantoization reduces memory proportionally to bits, with INT8 → 50%, INT4 → 25% of FP16 size
  • INT8 RTN works for most models with negligible quality loss
  • 4-bit requires sophisticated methods: GPTQ (optimal compensation) or AWQ (activation-aware scaling)
  • NF4 (normal float) gives better 4-bit quality than INT4 by matching weight distribution
  • QAT (quantization-aware training) recovers additional quality but requires retraining
