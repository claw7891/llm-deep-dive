Tokenization & Embeddings

How raw text becomes vectors that transformers can process

Before any attention happens or any parameter is updated, raw text must be converted into numerical form. This step — tokenization — is deceptively consequential: it defines the model's vocabulary, affects its ability to handle rare or unseen words, and can introduce subtle biases. Understanding the tokenizer is often the first diagnostic step when an LLM behaves unexpectedly.

The Tokenization Pipeline

Why do we need this?

Transformers are fundamentally numerical machines. They process matrices of floating-point numbers, not letters and spaces. So before the model can do anything useful with "The cat sat on the mat," we need to convert those characters into a structured numerical representation. This pipeline — text → tokens → token IDs → embeddings — is the front door of every LLM. Every word you send to ChatGPT has been through this exact process.

Here's the full pipeline at a glance:

Text → Tokens → Token IDs → Embeddings → Model Input

Subword Tokenization

Why do we need this?

There are three naive approaches to tokenization, and they all have problems:

❌ Word-level tokenization

If we made every full English word a token, we'd need hundreds of thousands of tokens to cover the dictionary — and we'd still miss compound words, slang, typos, and domain-specific terms. What happens when someone says "onomatopoeia"? The tokenizer has never seen it. The model gets a mysterious <UNK> (unknown) token and loses information. This is what older NLP systems did, and they struggled badly on any text outside their training data.

❌ Character-level tokenization

Every letter gets its own token. This solves the unknown-word problem — "onomatopoeia" is just 11 character tokens — but it creates a different disaster: the sentence "The cat sat on the mat" becomes 18 tokens instead of 6. Transformer attention scales quadratically with sequence length (O(n²)), so longer sequences mean dramatically more computation. A 2000-character paragraph would require ~4 million attention operations. Character-level models are painfully slow and struggle to capture long-range patterns.

✅ Subword tokenization — the Goldilocks solution

Subword tokenization splits text into the most common character sequences found in the training data. Common words stay intact ("the", "cat"), unknown words decompose into known pieces ("unhappiness" → "un" + "happiness"). The vocabulary stays manageable (30k-128k tokens) while sequence lengths remain reasonable (~3-5 characters per token). This is the approach used by every modern LLM.

Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE)

Used by GPT models. The algorithm iteratively merges the most frequent adjacent pair of tokens:

  1. Start with individual characters/bytes as the vocabulary
  2. Count all adjacent pairs in the training corpus
  3. Merge the most frequent pair into a new token
  4. Repeat until desired vocabulary size is reached (e.g., 50k, 100k)

Step-by-step: How BPE tokenizes "unhappiness"

Let's trace through what happens when a model that has been trained with BPE encounters the word "unhappiness" — a word that may or may not be in its vocabulary:

BPE Merge Walkthrough — "unhappiness"
Start: Each character is its own token:
u n h a p p i n e s s (11 tokens)

After common bigram merges learned during training:
Step 1: "p"+"p" → "pp"  (most frequent pair is always "pp" in any corpus with "happy")
u n h a [pp] i n e s s (10 tokens)

Step 2: "n"+"e" → "ne"  (common English bigram)
u n h a [pp] i [ne] s s (9 tokens)

Step 3: "s"+"s" → "ss"  (another highly frequent pair)
u n h a [pp] i [ne] [ss] (8 tokens)

Step 4: "ne"+"ss" → "ness"  (common suffix, frequent in training)
u n h a [pp] i [ness] (6 tokens)

Step 5: "un"+"h" → "unh"  (starts merging the prefix area)
[unh] a [pp] i [ness] (5 tokens)

Step 6: "a"+"pp" → "app"  (common in "happy", "application")
[unh] [app] i [ness] (4 tokens)

Step 7: "app"+"i" → "appi"  (less common, may not merge)
OR: "unh"+"app" → "unhapp"  (depends on training data)

Final: If vocabulary has "unhappiness" as a whole word (frequent enough), it ends as one token:
[unhappiness] (1 token)

If not, it might tokenize as "un" + "happiness" if both are common subwords,
or even fragment further: "un" + "happ" + "iness" for a smaller vocabulary.

The key insight: every word that can be broken into subword pieces will be. The model never sees an unknown token — it just sees fragments of the word. This is far better than the old <UNK> approach, because the fragments carry meaning too: "un" means negation, "happiness" means the state of being happy. The model learns this compositionally.

Canonical BPE Example from Literature

BPE Merge Example (Classic Corpus)
Corpus: "low low low low low lowest lowest lowest wider wider wider"

Step 1: l o w (3 tokens) + whitespace
Step 2: "lo" is most frequent pair → merge → token: lo
Step 3: "low" is most frequent → merge → token: low
Step 4: "lowest" = low + est → merge → token: lowest
Step 5: "wider" = wid + er → merge → token: wider

Result: Vocabulary = {l, o, w, e, s, t, i, d, r, lo, low, est, lowest, wid, er, wider}

WordPiece (BERT)

Similar to BPE but merges pairs that maximize the likelihood of the training data under a language model, rather than by raw frequency. The merge criterion is:

score(x, y) = P(xy) / (P(x) · P(y))

Where P(x) is the unigram probability of token x. Pairs that appear together more often than expected by chance get merged. This tends to produce cleaner linguistic subword units compared to BPE's purely frequency-based merges.

SentencePiece (LLaMA, Gemma)

Treats the input as a raw byte stream (no pretokenization needed), allowing it to handle any Unicode character, digit, or whitespace without language-specific preprocessing. Often uses the Unigram language model objective for segmentation, which considers all possible tokenizations of a sentence and picks the most likely one.

Vocabulary Size & Compression

Vocabulary size is a key architectural decision that impacts both model quality and efficiency:

GPT-4cl100k_base (BPE)100,256~4.0 chars/token

Compression ratio — characters per token — measures how efficiently the tokenizer packs information. Higher compression means fewer tokens for the same text, which reduces sequence length and thus attention computation cost (which scales O(L²)). LLaMA 3's larger vocabulary (128k tokens) is a deliberate design choice: more tokens means better compression, which means shorter sequences for the same amount of text. A 1,000-character passage takes ~192 LLaMA 3 tokens vs ~286 GPT-2 tokens — saving ~33% in sequence length.

Common Tokenization Pitfalls

Why do we need to care about this?

Tokenization might seem like a mechanical detail, but it has real, measurable effects on model behavior. Understanding these pitfalls helps you diagnose why an LLM might fail on seemingly simple inputs.

The "IndianaJones" Problem

Consider what happens when GPT-2 tokenizes the same name with and without a space:

Tokenization Comparison
"Indiana Jones"  →  tokenizes as [Indiana] [Jones] — two whole words, each known to the model.

"IndianaJones"  →  tokenizes as [Ind] [iana] [Jones] — three fragments, one of which ("Ind") is misleading.

Result: The model processes these two inputs through completely different pathways. "Indiana" is a well-understood concept; "Ind" is a fragment that might evoke "Independence," "Indiana," or "Index" — all at once. This is why LLMs sometimes behave unexpectedly when you remove spaces or use camelCase, and it's also why code (which rarely uses spaces around operators) can be harder for LLMs to reason about.

Multilingual Tokenization Bias

Most tokenizers are trained primarily on English text. This creates a systematic bias against other languages:

  • English: "Hello, how are you?" → 5 tokens (efficient, ~4 chars/token)
  • Spanish: "Hola, ¿cómo estás?" → 8 tokens (lower efficiency due to accented characters)
  • Mandarin: "你好，你好吗？" → 10+ tokens (each character may be split into multiple byte-level tokens)
  • Thai: "สวัสดีครับ" → 15+ tokens (no spaces between words means every character competes for space in the vocabulary)

This means a model's effective context window is shorter for non-English languages — the same amount of information takes more tokens. GPT-4's 8k context might feel like 2k for a Thai user. This is an active area of research, with newer models (Gemma, LLaMA 3) using larger vocabularies and more multilingual training data to mitigate the issue.

Tokenization Artifacts

Tokenization artifacts: BPE can split unexpected words in ways that affect model performance. For example, "cowboy" might be tokenized as "cow" + "boy" (which is sensible) or "c" + "owboy" (problematic). Words seen fewer times in training get fragmented more, which is why LLMs struggle with unusual names, technical jargon, and code. The fundamental issue is that the tokenizer makes irreversible decisions about what counts as a "unit of meaning," and those decisions aren't always aligned with human intuition.

Token Embeddings

Why do we need this?

At this point, we have token IDs — integers like [464, 824, 1089]. These are just labels; they carry no inherent meaning. The number 464 doesn't tell the model anything about the word "The." We need to convert these IDs into information-rich vectors that capture semantic relationships. That's the job of the embedding matrix.

Each token ID is mapped to a dense vector via an embedding matrix E ∈ ℝV×d, where V is vocabulary size and d is the embedding dimension (typically 768–8192).

xi = E[ti]

The embedding matrix is learned from scratch during pretraining. Each row of E is a trainable parameter representing one token in d-dimensional space. Semantically similar tokens (e.g., "king" and "queen") end up with similar vectors — the model learns this organically from co-occurrence patterns in text.

What does an embedding vector actually look like?

Real embeddings are high-dimensional (768 to 8192 dimensions), but we can build intuition by looking at a simplified 3-dimensional version:

Example: Simplified 3D Embedding Vectors
We have a tiny vocabulary of 3 tokens, each projected into 3 dimensions. A trained embedding matrix might look like:

E = [   [0.82, -0.53, 0.21],   # token "king"
  [0.71, -0.45, 0.31],   # token "queen"
  [0.15, -0.92, 0.08]   # token "table"
]

Notice that king and queen have similar coordinates: both have large positive x-values and negative y-values. They're neighbors in vector space. Table is far away (very negative y, small x).

If we compute the distance between:
 • ‖king − queen‖ ≈ √((0.82−0.71)² + (−0.53+0.45)² + (0.21−0.31)²) = 0.14 (close)
 • ‖king − table‖ ≈ √((0.82−0.15)² + (−0.53+0.92)² + (0.21−0.08)²) = 0.76 (far)

In real models with 768+ dimensions, this semantic geometry becomes remarkably rich. Words cluster by topic, by part of speech, by sentiment — all learned without any explicit supervision.
Embedding arithmetic: The classic example is king − man + woman ≈ queen. While less clean for subword tokenizers, high-quality embeddings still encode rich semantic relationships in their vector geometry. The embedding space learns analogies, pluralization, and even verb tenses from co-occurrence statistics alone.

Positional Encodings

Why do we need this?

The self-attention mechanism processes all tokens in parallel — it calculates attention scores based purely on the content of each token, not their positions. If you fed the model "The cat sat on the mat" or "mat the on sat cat The," the attention mechanism would see them as identical. But word order matters enormously in language: "dog bites man" means something very different from "man bites dog."

Think of it like seat numbers in a theater. The model is an audience watching the tokens perform on a stage. Self-attention tells the model which performers are interacting, but it needs seat numbers to know who's sitting where. Without positional encodings, the model sees a crowded stage with no sense of left vs right or who follows whom.

Sinusoidal (Original Transformer)

Positional Encoding Formula
PE(pos, 2i) = sin(pos / 100002i/d)
PE(pos, 2i+1) = cos(pos / 100002i/d)

Each position gets a unique encoding. Different dimensions have different frequencies — some dimensions oscillate rapidly (capturing neighboring word relationships) while others oscillate slowly (capturing long-range document structure). The key mathematical property: the encoding for position pos+k can be expressed as a linear function of the encoding for position pos, which makes it easy for the model to learn relative offsets.

RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding)

Used in LLaMA, Mistral, Gemma. Instead of adding positional vectors, RoPE rotates the query and key vectors by an angle proportional to position:

qi ← R(θ · pos) · qi     kj ← R(θ · pos) · kj

Why rotation encodes relative position

Imagine you have two vectors on a clock face. The hour hand is at 3 o'clock, the minute hand is at 12. Their relative angle is 90°. Now rotate the entire clock by 30° — the hour hand goes to 4, the minute hand goes to 1. Their relative angle is still 90°.

This is exactly what RoPE does. It rotates both the query vector at position i and the key vector at position j by their respective position angles. When you compute the dot product qi · kj, the rotation angles don't cancel — instead, the dot product naturally equals q · k rotated by (posi − posj). The absolute positions vanish, leaving only the relative distance between tokens.

RoPE Intuition
Without rotation: q·k captures content similarity only — no positional information.
With RoPE:
 1. Rotate q at position 5 by angle θ·5
 2. Rotate k at position 9 by angle θ·9
 3. The dot product q5·k9 = q·k scaled by cos(θ·|5−9|)

Result: The attention score between token 5 and token 9 depends on their distance (4 positions apart), not on their absolute positions. This is why RoPE models can generalize to sequence lengths beyond what they were trained on — they've learned to attend based on "how far away" rather than "where in the document."

This relative-position property is a major advantage. Models using RoPE (like LLaMA 3) can be evaluated on 8k, 32k, or even 128k token sequences even though they were trained on shorter sequences, because the positional encoding itself doesn't break down at unseen positions.

ALiBi (Attention with Linear Biases)

Instead of modifying embeddings, ALiBi adds a bias to attention scores based on the distance between tokens:

score(qi, kj) = qi · kj − m · |i − j|

Where m is a head-specific slope. Simpler than RoPE, used in BLOOM and some MPT models. The idea is brutally simple: penalize attention between distant tokens by a fixed amount proportional to their distance. This biases the model toward local context (nearby tokens matter more) while still allowing long-range attention when the content strongly matches.

Summary

The journey from text to model input involves:

  • Subword tokenization (BPE, WordPiece, SentencePiece) balancing vocabulary size and compression — the Goldilocks solution between word-level and character-level approaches
  • Token IDs mapped through a learned embedding matrix to dense vectors that capture semantic geometry in high-dimensional space
  • Positional encodings (sinusoidal, RoPE, ALiBi) injecting sequence order information — seat numbers for the theater of attention
  • The resulting d×n tensor (n tokens, d dimensions) is the transformer input — a numerical representation ready for the attention mechanism
