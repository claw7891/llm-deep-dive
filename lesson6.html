Scaling Laws

Empirical relationships between compute, data, and model performance

One of the most important empirical discoveries in deep learning is that the performance of LLMs follows predictable power-law relationships with compute, model size, and data volume. These scaling laws aren't just academic — they directly determine how to allocate resources, whether to train larger models or use more data, and they explain why the field has shifted toward trillion-parameter models and multi-trillion-token datasets.
TL;DR

LLM loss follows predictable power-law scaling: L ∝ N−α (parameters), L ∝ D−β (data), and L ∝ C−γ (compute). For compute-optimal training, model size and data should scale together — the Chinchilla scaling law found most models were undertrained. Larger models are more sample-efficient (learn more per token), but the gains diminish according to predictable exponents.

Kaplan et al. (2020) Scaling Laws

The foundational paper trained hundreds of transformer models from 1M to 1.7B parameters on various data budgets and found three clean power-law relationships:

Power-Law Relationships
L(N) = (N0 / N)αN     (performance vs parameters)
L(D) = (D0 / D)αD     (performance vs data)
L(C) = (C0 / C)αC     (performance vs compute)

Where αN ≈ 0.076, αD ≈ 0.093, αC ≈ 0.050

Key findings:

  • Performance improves smoothly and predictably with scale — no "breakthrough" thresholds
  • Larger models are more sample-efficient: they use fewer tokens to reach the same loss
  • For a given compute budget, the optimal allocation is to train much larger models than was conventional at the time
  • Transfer performance (fine-tuning) follows similar scaling to pretraining loss
📈 Scaling Law: Loss vs Parameters for Different Data Budgets

Cross-entropy loss decreases as model parameters increase. More training data (higher D) lowers the entire curve. Key insight: a 7B model trained on 3T tokens outperforms a 70B model trained on only 30B tokens.

Chinchilla Scaling (2022)

Hoffmann et al. at DeepMind re-examined scaling laws and found that Kaplan et al. overestimated the benefit of scaling parameters relative to data. Their key result:

Chinchilla optimality: For a given compute budget, the optimal number of parameters N* and training tokens D* follows N* ∝ D* ∝ √C, meaning they should be scaled equally. For every doubling of compute, both model size and training tokens should double.
Compute-Optimal Allocation
L(N, D) = E + A · N−α + B · D−β

Where E = irreducible loss (≈ 1.69 nats for their data)
A = 406.4, B = 410.7, α = 0.34, β = 0.28

For compute C ≈ 6ND:
Nopt = (γ · C) / (6 · (1 + γ)) where γ = αβ / βα
Dopt = C / (6 · Nopt)

Practical implication: most existing large models (GPT-3, Gopher, etc.) were significantly undertrained — they would have achieved better performance at the same compute cost by training on more data with fewer parameters.

Practical Implications

Pre-Chinchilla ApproachPost-Chinchilla Approach
Train to convergence (8-20 epochs)Single epoch on diverse data
Model size matters more than dataModel size and data scale together
GPT-3: 175B on 300B tokens (ratio ~3:1)LLaMA: 7B on 1T tokens (ratio ~1:150)
Overtraining is acceptableOvertraining degrades generalization

The LLaMA series exemplifies the post-Chinchilla philosophy: train relatively small models on massive token budgets. LLaMA 7B on 1T tokens rivals GPT-3 175B on many benchmarks — demonstrating that data scaling is more impactful than parameter scaling, at least up to the Chinchilla-optimal point.

Emergent Abilities

While pretraining loss follows smooth power laws, downstream capabilities often appear abruptly at specific scales:

Emergent vs Smooth Scaling

Examples of emergent abilities (not present in models below a threshold):

  • Chain-of-thought reasoning (~100B+)
  • Word-level arithmetic (~10B+)
  • In-context learning with high precision (~10B+)
  • Code generation (~50B+)
  • Multi-step mathematical reasoning (~100B+)
Debate on emergence: Some researchers argue that emergent abilities are an artifact of discontinuous evaluation metrics. When measured with continuous metrics (e.g., probability of correct token), many supposedly emergent abilities show smooth scaling. The true nature of emergence remains an active research question.

The Irreducible Loss

Scaling laws have an asymptote — the irreducible loss E, which represents the entropy of natural language itself. Even with infinite compute and data, the model can't predict the next token perfectly because language is inherently uncertain:

L∞ = H(language) ≈ 1.5-2.5 nats/token (varies by vocabulary and domain)

The gap between a model's loss and this irreducible loss determines how much room for improvement remains. Current best models (GPT-4, Claude 3) are approaching within ~0.3 nats of the estimated irreducible loss, suggesting that pure scaling of current architectures may yield diminishing returns.
