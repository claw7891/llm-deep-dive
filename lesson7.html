Alignment & Instruction Tuning

From raw internet-text predictor to helpful, harmless assistant

A raw pretrained LLM completes sentences — it doesn't answer questions helpfully. It might continue "What is the capital of France?" with "is a question that many people ask" rather than "Paris." Alignment is the process of reshaping the model's behavior to be helpful, honest, and harmless. It's a fundamentally different training paradigm — instead of maximizing likelihood of training data, we're optimizing for human preferences.
TL;DR

Alignment has three main stages: (1) Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) — train on human-written instruction-response pairs to teach format and style; (2) Reward Modeling — train a separate model to predict human preferences; (3) Reinforcement Learning (RLHF/PPO) — use the reward model to further optimize the LLM's policy. DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) achieves similar results without the separate reward model by directly optimizing on pairwise preference data.

Before alignment: prompt='What is 2+2?' → '2+2 is a mathematical operation that combines two numbers...' (continues indefinitely)
After SFT: '2+2 is 4.'
After RLHF: '2+2 equals 4. Would you like help with any other calculations?'

Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

The first alignment step. Collect a dataset of high-quality instruction-response pairs (typically 10k-100k examples) and fine-tune the model with standard cross-entropy loss:

LSFT = −Σi log Pθ(responsei | instructioni)

SFT teaches the model the format of a helpful conversation — that it should answer questions, acknowledge uncertainty, and follow instructions. But it has limitations:

  • Quality depends entirely on the SFT dataset — bad demonstrations teach bad behavior
  • Models learn to mimic the style of demonstrations without deep understanding
  • SFT data is expensive to collect (human-written, quality-controlled)
Concrete SFT example: Instruction: 'Write a poem about a cat.' Response: 'Whiskers twitching, eyes so bright,/Pouncing softly through the night...' The model learns this OUTPUT style from observing thousands of pairs. It learns format (stop generating after answering), not just content.

The key insight: SFT alone is necessary but not sufficient. The model learns format but not preference — it can't distinguish a good answer from a great one.

Stage 2: Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

RLHF goes beyond imitation to directly optimize for human preferences:

RLHF Pipeline
θ(x, y) 3. Optimize LLM (PPO) πθ via PPO Preference label Reward signal Policy update

Reward Model Training

Train a separate model rφ(x, y) — typically initialized from the SFT model with the language modeling head replaced by a scalar head — to predict human preferences. For a pair of responses (y1, y2) given input x, where y1 is preferred over y2:

Reward Model Loss (Bradley-Terry)
Loss = −log σ(rφ(x, y1) − rφ(x, y2))

Where σ is the sigmoid function.
The model learns to assign higher scores to preferred responses.
Concrete reward model example: For prompt 'Explain quantum computing to a 5-year-old,' the RM might score: response A (simple analogy) = 8.5, response B (technical jargon) = 2.1, response C (rude) = -1.3. The RM learns to distinguish these by training on 100k+ human preference comparisons.

Typically trained on ~100k-1M human preference comparisons. The reward model should generalize to responses it hasn't seen — predicting what humans would prefer.

Policy Optimization (PPO)

Use the reward model to fine-tune the LLM policy πθ. At each step, sample a response from the current policy and score it with the reward model:

PPO Objective (simplified)
LPPO = Ey ∼ πθ[ rφ(x, y) − β · KL(πθ || πref) ]

Where:
rφ(x, y) = reward model score
KL penalty prevents the policy from diverging too far from the SFT model
β controls the strength of the KL penalty (typically 0.01-0.1)
Concrete KL penalty example: Without KL penalty: the model discovers that starting responses with 'I am an AI assistant' gets a +0.5 reward bonus. After 1000 steps, every response starts with this phrase — it's exploited the reward model. The KL penalty prevents this by penalizing divergence from the original SFT model.

The KL penalty is essential. Without it, the policy can learn to exploit the reward model — finding responses that score highly but are gibberish or contain artifacts. The KL constraint keeps the optimized policy close to the well-behaved SFT model while still improving according to the reward signal.

DPO: Direct Preference Optimization

DPO eliminates the need for a separate reward model by directly optimizing the policy on preference pairs:

DPO Loss
LDPO = −log σ(β · log(πθ(yw|x) / πref(yw|x)) − β · log(πθ(yl|x) / πref(yl|x)))

Where yw = preferred response, yl = dispreferred response, πref is the reference (SFT) model.
DPO intuition: Instead of training a separate judge (reward model) and then optimizing against it, DPO directly increases the probability of preferred responses and decreases the probability of dispreferred ones, with a constraint to stay close to the original model.

Intuition: increase the relative probability of preferred responses vs dispreferred responses, while staying close to the reference model. DPO is simpler, cheaper, and often matches or exceeds RLHF quality — making it the preferred method in many recent alignment pipelines.

The Alignment Tax

Alignment often degrades performance on capability benchmarks — the model becomes more helpful and harmless but worse at math, coding, or reasoning. This is thought to happen because:

  • The alignment dataset biases the model toward certain response styles that aren't optimal for all tasks
  • The KL penalty prevents the model from exploring better reasoning strategies
  • Preference data might favor plausible-sounding wrong answers over correct but dry ones
Real numbers for alignment tax: LLaMA 2 7B base: MMLU 46.7%. LLaMA 2 7B Chat (aligned): MMLU 45.3%. A 1.4% drop. On math (GSM8K): 14.6% → 11.2% — a more significant drop. The model became more helpful and safe but lost some reasoning ability.

Mitigations include: balanced capability retention data during alignment, rejection sampling (use a stronger model to score candidates), and multi-task learning that mixes alignment and pretraining objectives.
